# The Tastemaker Point of View

> The spine the whole ladder hangs from. Tastemaker is an evaluator of taste — and a taste evaluator that refuses to commit to a point of view fails its own highest test. So here is the point of view, held with conviction.

---

## The thesis

**Taste is the act of deciding. No, not that, over and over, until what's left feels intentional. Its opposite isn't ugly design. It's design with no decisions in it: the default, the template, the safe choice that offends no one and says nothing.**

That is the whole position. Everything in the KB, the ladder, and the workflow is downstream of it.

Taste is not a *look*. It is not muted palettes, generous whitespace, large radii, or any other aesthetic — those are styles, and styling is what generation tools configure. Taste is the **act of choosing** — "no, not that" over and over until what's left actually means something. A design has taste when you can see the decisions in it: the things that were cut, the one thing made loud while everything else stayed quiet, the edge case that got the same care as the happy path, the convention kept on purpose and the convention broken on purpose. A design lacks taste when you can see no decision at all — when it is the average of its category, the framework default left untouched, the safe center reached for to avoid being disliked.

This is why the framing word is **intentionality**, not "taste." Taste is class-loaded and vague; intentionality is precise and democratic. Anyone can decide. The question is whether they did.

---

## The will and the eye

Deciding is half of it. The other half is knowing what's worth deciding toward — and that's the part Steve Jobs never let go of:

> *"It comes down to trying to expose yourself to the best things that humans have done, and then trying to bring those things into what you're doing."*

You can decide ferociously and still be wrong. Conviction without exposure is just a confident bad call. A designer who has never seen great work will commit hard to the mediocre, because the mediocre is all they know to reach for. So taste needs two things: the **will** to decide (refuse the default) and the **eye** to decide *well* (a wide enough vocabulary of good work to know what's worth committing to). The will is the courage; the eye is the calibration.

This manifesto is the will — *decide, refuse the default*. **The KB is the eye.** Every CR rule and TS principle, and especially the exemplars drawn across distinct design traditions (editorial, consumer-warm, dense-pro, utilitarian, dev-tool craft), exist to train the eye on what good looks like — so a decision is informed, not just confident. The two are inseparable: the headline of this document tells you to decide; the rest of the KB teaches you to decide well. A taste evaluation that only checked for conviction would reward confident bad work. We check for both.

---

## What this commits us to — and what it refuses

The point of view is **fierce about deciding and neutral about the aesthetic.** This is the single distinction that makes it a spine rather than a style cop.

**Fierce about deciding:**
- The top of the ladder (Convicted) is "commits to a point of view, feels inevitable, willing to risk being disliked." That is intentionality at maximum. It is *definitionally* the highest taste.
- The bottom of bad taste is the default, the template, the hedge — the refusal to decide. The named anti-patterns (generic agency template, gradient-as-brand, the average of the category) are all the same failure: nobody chose.
- We grade *down* on absence of decision with full confidence. "This commits to nothing" is not a hedge; it is the sharpest verdict the tool can deliver.

**Neutral about the aesthetic — the anti-dogma guard:**
- **Paula Scher's expressive maximalism passes.** It is wildly intentional — every loud choice is a choice. That it is not restrained is irrelevant; it decided.
- **Dieter Rams's minimalism passes.** It is wildly intentional — every removal is a choice. That it is quiet is irrelevant; it decided.
- A design that is loud-because-it-decided-to-be-loud and a design that is quiet-because-it-decided-to-be-quiet are *both tasteful.* A design that is quiet-because-quiet-is-the-default is not.
- We do **not** grade a design down for being expressive, maximalist, dense, playful, or loud. We grade it down only for being *undecided*.

The test for whether the POV has slipped into dogma: **could a maximalist design score Tasteful?** If the answer is ever "no, because it's not restrained," the tool has become a style cop and is wrong. The answer must always be "yes, if it committed."

---

## How it answers the standing critiques

This POV was chosen specifically because it survives three recurring critiques of taste frameworks:

1. **"Taste launders class into talent" (Zhou, via Bourdieu).** Intentionality doesn't. It is not correlated with resources, education, or a particular cultural canon. A scrappy, weird, fully-committed indie design has more taste, by this definition, than a polished template. The POV is *about the decision*, which anyone can make — not about the reference set, which only some can afford.
2. **"Codifying taste produces the monoculture it warns against."** A POV whose highest principle is *refuse the default* cannot, in being applied, mandate a default — unless applied badly. The anti-dogma guard (Scher and Rams both pass) is the structural defense: we reward divergence in *what* you decide, requiring only *that* you decide.
3. **"Taste is shallow / lipstick on pigs."** Answered by the two assumed floors — we judge how well an idea is realized, not whether it was worth realizing (strategy) or whether it works (usability). Intentionality is about the realization, honestly scoped.

---

## How it maps onto the ladder

The ladder is the thesis, operationalized as altitude:

| Rung | Intentionality reading |
|------|------------------------|
| **Correct** | The floor — usable, no broken tells. Below intentionality; assumed. |
| **Coherent** | One mind made these decisions. The parts agree because they were *chosen* to. The first rung where intentionality is visible. |
| **Characterful** | The decisions add up to a *specific identity* — you can ID it from a crop. Intentionality made into a recognizable voice. |
| **Convicted** | Maximal intentionality — commits to an opinion strong enough to risk being disliked. The default is not just avoided; it is *refused on principle*. |

And the ceiling (Contextual Fit) is itself an intentional choice: a checkout that *decides* to stay conventional is intentional restraint, not a failure to decide. The POV holds even here — the tasteful checkout *chose* convention; the tasteless one *defaulted* to it. The difference is whether a decision was made, which is the only thing we ever measure.

---

## The tool must pass its own test

A taste evaluator that hedges — "this is one valid view among many" — is reaching for the safe center to avoid being disliked. That is the exact failure it grades others down for. So tastemaker holds this POV with conviction, names it as a stance when it matters, and does not apologize for it. The anti-dogma guard is not a hedge; it is the *content* of the conviction — the POV is precisely "decide, and I won't tell you what to decide."

That is a real opinion, defensible and falsifiable (could a maximalist pass? yes). It is the tool, passing its own Convicted rung.
