# Tension and Release

## Rule
Across a long layout, alternate **dense and breathing** passages rather than holding one uniform density top to bottom. A dense, information-rich section earns a spacious, simple one after it. This is the macro-rhythm of a page — the pacing of busy and calm.

## Why it matters
Uniform density — every section equally packed, or equally sparse — reads as monotonous; the eye has no rhythm to follow and no place to rest. Alternating tension (dense, detailed) and release (spacious, singular) gives a page tempo, the way a piece of music does. It also directs attention: a calm section after a dense one makes the next thing land. This is what separates a *composed* page from a *stacked* one.

## How to spot it
Scroll the full layout and feel the tempo. Tells of missing rhythm: every section the same compositional weight (the "wall of equal cards" feeling); no breathing room between dense passages; or, conversely, relentless density with no rest. Squint — if all sections blur to the same texture, there's no rhythm.

## The fix
Vary section composition: follow a dense feature grid with a single-focus full-width moment; give a heavy data section a spacious header. Don't repeat the same layout-density for every section (this also relates to varying layout families across a page).

## Eye-training
Look at a well-composed long page (a strong editorial site, a well-made landing page) and notice the *breathing* — how it tightens and opens as you scroll. Then look at a monotonous one and feel the flatness. Train the sense of "tempo" as a thing a layout has or lacks.

## When to flex
A **heuristic**, not a law — many excellent surfaces (a focused tool, a single-purpose screen) are legitimately uniform and shouldn't manufacture artificial rhythm. Tension-and-release matters most for long, scrolling, multi-section layouts (marketing pages, long-form). Short, single-job screens don't need it.
